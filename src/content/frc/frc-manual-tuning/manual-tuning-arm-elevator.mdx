The [turret recipe](/frc/manual-tuning-turret) with a gravity step in front, and the gravity step has a trick in it that also finds `kS`. This is the procedure for the mechanisms that fall when you let go: arms, pivots, hoods, elevators, climbers.

The arm procedure is the turret procedure with a gravity step in front, and the gravity step has a trick in it that also finds `kS`. The example: a 35:1 arm in `PositionTorqueCurrentFOC` with `GravityType = Arm_Cosine`.

<StepsBox title="Arm (or elevator) procedure">

1. **Zero all gains.**
2. **Find the smallest `kG` at which the arm stops falling.** Raise it until the arm holds; nudge down until it just falls again. This is the *lower* bound: it equals gravity minus friction, `kG − kS`, because friction is helping hold it.
3. **Find the largest `kG` at which the arm does not rise.** Keep raising past the hold point until the arm starts to climb; nudge down until it just stops. This is the *upper* bound: gravity plus friction, `kG + kS`.
4. **`kG` is the midpoint** of the two: `((kG + kS) + (kG − kS)) ÷ 2`.
5. **`kS` is half the difference**: `((kG + kS) − (kG − kS)) ÷ 2`. One bracketing experiment has measured both gravity and friction.
6. **Set a nearby setpoint** (0.1 rotations).
7. **Raise `kP` until significant overshoot.**
8. **Raise `kD` until it stops.**
9. **Repeat 7–8** to the latency limit, exactly as for the [turret](/frc/manual-tuning-turret), then back off `kD` and `kP` in that order.
10. **Verify at other setpoints**, including the other direction.

</StepsBox>

For an **elevator** the same bracket applies with `GravityType = Elevator_Static`, and it is even easier to read: the carriage sits still between the two bounds. Do the bracket at mid-travel. For an arm, do it **horizontal**, where `cos θ = 1` and the gravity term is at its maximum ([Feedforward](/frc/feedforward#the-three-equations)).

**How the worked example went.** `kG` 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 20 (holds), 18, 16 (falls), 17 (falls appreciably), 17.5 (holds) → lower bound 17.5. Up again: 20, 22, 24, 26 (climbs), 25 (climbs), 24.5 (holds) → upper bound 24.5. So `kG = (24.5 + 17.5) ÷ 2 = 21 A` and `kS = (24.5 − 17.5) ÷ 2 = 3.5 A`. Then the `kP`/`kD` ladder: `kP` 1 → 16 (overshoot), `kD` 1 → 8; `kP` 32, 64, `kD` 16, 32; `kP` 128, 250, 500, `kD` 64, 128; `kP` 1000, 2000 (a little oscillation), `kD` 250; `kP` 4000, 8000, `kD` 500, which produced on-the-way oscillation: the limit. `kD` back to 400, 300. A 0.6-rotation setpoint overshot and `kD` could not rise, so `kP` 4000, 3700. Checked at −0.1 and 0.4. **Final: `kG = 21 A`, `kS = 3.5 A`, `kP = 3700 A/rot`, `kD = 300 A/rps`.**

This is the procedure behind [PID Tuning Practice – Elevator](/frc/pid-tuning-practice-elevator) and [– Arm](/frc/pid-tuning-practice-arm) (voltage mode, so volts throughout and much smaller `kP`), and the numbers in FRC 4451's [Elevator Example](/frc/elevator-example) were found this way.

<RulesBox title="Common mistakes">

- **Bracketing an arm somewhere other than horizontal.** The gravity term is largest at horizontal; that is where the bracket measures it.
- **Taking the first hold point as <code>kG</code>.** That is <code>kG − kS</code>. Find the upper bound too and take the midpoint.
- **Forgetting the other direction.** Verify moves both up and down, and a far setpoint as well as a near one.
- **Accepting overshoot on an arm with hard stops.** Trade a little overdamping on small moves for no overshoot on large ones, as the example does.

</RulesBox>
