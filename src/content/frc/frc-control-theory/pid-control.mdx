Every mechanism on the robot that is told to be somewhere (an elevator at a height, a flywheel at a speed, a swerve module at an angle) is running a **feedback loop**: measure where it is, compare with where it should be, push in proportion to the difference, repeat fifty times a second. **PID** is the most common way to turn that difference into a push. It is worth understanding once, properly, because the same three numbers appear in WPILib, in Phoenix, in REVLib, in the tuning-practice sessions and in every mechanism example that follows.

Kit Bot deliberately stopped short of this: [Kit Bot R09 Parts 1 and 2](/kit-bot/r09-characterization) introduced SysId testing, checked the fitted model, and recorded feedforward gains with their units. This lesson is the part it left out.

### The loop

<ReferenceImage
  src="/media/frc/pid-block-diagram.png"
  alt="WPILib feedback-control block diagram with a controller, plant, sensor, setpoint, and measured output"
  caption="A feedback controller compares the requested state with the measured state, then changes its output to reduce the error."
  sourceUrl="https://docs.wpilib.org/en/stable/docs/software/advanced-controls/introduction/introduction-to-pid.html"
  sourceLabel="WPILib: Introduction to PID"
/>

```text
setpoint ──►( − )──► error ──► [ controller ] ──► output ──► [ mechanism ] ──► measurement
              ▲                                                                  │
              └──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

Three words that every later page uses precisely:

- **Setpoint:** where the mechanism should be right now. With a motion profile this changes every loop; without one it is the goal.
- **Error:** `setpoint − measurement`, in the mechanism's units (inches, rotations, rotations per second).
- **Output:** what the controller sends to the motor: a voltage, a duty cycle (−1 to 1), or a torque current in amps. The units of the output decide the units of the gains, which is why a `kP` from one robot means nothing on another.

### The three terms

The controller's output is a sum of three contributions, each computed from the error in a different way:

<ContentTable
  headers={["Term", "Computes", "Answers", "Too little", "Too much"]}
  rows={[
    ["<strong>P</strong>, proportional", "<code>kP × error</code>", "How far away am I?", "Slow, never quite arrives", "Overshoots, then oscillates around the setpoint"],
    ["<strong>I</strong>, integral", "<code>kI × Σ(error × dt)</code>", "How long have I been short?", "A small, permanent offset remains", "Winds up during a long move and overshoots badly at the end"],
    ["<strong>D</strong>, derivative", "<code>kD × d(error)/dt</code>", "How fast is the error changing?", "P overshoots with nothing to damp it", "Twitchy, amplifies sensor noise, can buzz the motor"]
  ]}
/>

A worked example makes the sizes concrete. An elevator in **inches**, output in **volts**, `kP = 0.5 V/in`, `kD = 0`, `kI = 0`:

<ContentTable
  headers={["Error", "P output", "What happens"]}
  rows={[
    ["10 in below", "+5.0 V", "Drives up hard"],
    ["2 in below", "+1.0 V", "Slowing as it approaches. This is why P alone does not overshoot much on a slow mechanism"],
    ["0.5 in below", "+0.25 V", "Not enough to overcome friction, and not enough to hold the carriage against gravity: the elevator <em>stops short</em>"],
    ["0 in", "0 V", "Being an elevator, it then falls until the error is big enough to hold it: a steady sag of a few inches"]
  ]}
/>

That last row is the whole reason the next lesson exists. The integral term would eventually accumulate enough to lift it, but slowly and with overshoot; the right answer is a **gravity feedforward** that supplies the holding voltage without any error at all, leaving P to handle only the transient ([Feedforward](/frc/feedforward)). At FRC 4451 `kI` is rarely used. A mechanism that seems to need it is usually missing a feedforward term.

`kD` is the damper. When the mechanism is racing toward the setpoint the error is shrinking fast, `d(error)/dt` is large and negative, and the D term subtracts from the push, braking before the overshoot rather than after. It is the term to add when a well-fed-forward mechanism still oscillates, and the term to reduce when the output looks jagged on a graph.

### Where the loop runs

The loop can execute in your code on the roboRIO, or on the motor controller itself. Both are PID; they differ in rate, in visibility, and in how you write them.

<ContentTable
  headers={["", "On the RIO: WPILib <code>PIDController</code>", "On the controller: Phoenix Slot0 / REV closed loop"]}
  rows={[
    ["Rate", "Once per robot loop, 50 Hz (20 ms)", "1 kHz on a TalonFX; the measurement never goes over CAN"],
    ["Output", "You get a number back and decide what to do with it", "The controller drives the motor directly"],
    ["Debugging", "Every intermediate value is a Java variable you can log", "You read back the closed-loop reference, error and output as status signals"],

    ["Team usage", "Software-loop sim IOs, the drivetrain's heading controller, path followers", "Every mechanism through the ROBOTZ Garage library's <code>…IOTalonFX</code>"]
  ]}
/>

<CodeTabs>
<CodeTab label="On the RIO (WPILib)">

```java
import edu.wpi.first.math.controller.PIDController;

private final PIDController pid = new PIDController(0.5, 0.0, 0.02);   // kP, kI, kD, in volts per inch here

@Override
public void periodic() {
  double volts = pid.calculate(getHeightInches(), goalInches);        // measurement, setpoint
  io.runVolts(MathUtil.clamp(volts, -12, 12));
}
```

`calculate(measurement, setpoint)` returns the output in whatever units your gains are in; nothing is applied until you apply it. Useful extras: `setTolerance` and `atSetpoint()` for a readiness `Trigger`; `enableContinuousInput(-Math.PI, Math.PI)` for anything that wraps, like a heading or a turret; `setIntegratorRange` to stop wind-up if you do use `kI`.

</CodeTab>
<CodeTab label="On the controller (Phoenix 6)">

```java
import com.ctre.phoenix6.configs.Slot0Configs;
import com.ctre.phoenix6.controls.PositionVoltage;

// once, in the config the IO applies
new Slot0Configs().withKP(4.0).withKI(0.0).withKD(0.1);   // volts per mechanism rotation, because the request below is a Voltage one

// every time the goal changes
talon.setControl(new PositionVoltage(goalRotations));
```

The gains live in a **slot** on the device and the control request says which slot and which output unit to use. `PositionVoltage` makes them volts per rotation; `PositionTorqueCurrentFOC` would make the same fields amps per rotation ([Control Requests](/frc/control-requests) is the full catalogue). In the ROBOTZ Garage library the slot is a `Slot0Configs` inside a `MotorConfigs` record and the request is chosen by the IO, so you never write these lines in a subsystem ([Motor Controller Configuration](/frc/motor-controller-configuration)).

</CodeTab>
<CodeTab label="On the controller (REVLib)">

```java
import com.revrobotics.spark.config.SparkMaxConfig;
import com.revrobotics.spark.SparkBase.ControlType;

SparkMaxConfig config = new SparkMaxConfig();
config.closedLoop.p(0.1).i(0.0).d(0.01);                 // duty cycle per rotation by default
motor.configure(config, ResetMode.kResetSafeParameters, PersistMode.kPersistParameters);

motor.getClosedLoopController().setReference(goalRotations, ControlType.kPosition);
```

Same idea, different names. Note the output unit: SPARK closed loops produce a **duty cycle** unless configured otherwise, so a `kP` here is roughly a twelfth of the equivalent voltage-mode gain.

</CodeTab>
</CodeTabs>

### Tuning

<ReferenceImage
  src="/media/frc/pid-response-types.png"
  alt="WPILib plots comparing underdamped, critically damped, and overdamped responses to a setpoint"
  caption="These response shapes give tuning words a visible meaning: oscillation is underdamped, while a slow approach is overdamped."
  sourceUrl="https://docs.wpilib.org/en/stable/docs/software/advanced-controls/introduction/introduction-to-pid.html"
  sourceLabel="WPILib: Introduction to PID"
/>

Tune **feedforward first**, then PID. Every PID gain found before the feedforward is in place is compensating for a missing physical term and will be wrong afterwards. The full measurement procedure is in [Feedforward](/frc/feedforward#measuring-instead-of-guessing); with that done, the feedback part is short. Log the setpoint and the measurement on one graph ([commanded versus measured](/frc/simulation-in-advantagescope#the-line-graph-commanded-versus-measured)) and:

<StepsBox title="Tuning the feedback gains">

1. **Start with `kP` small and `kI = kD = 0`.** Command a step. Double `kP` until the response overshoots, then back off to between the last two values. On a mechanism whose feedforward is right this converges in a handful of tries.
2. **If it oscillates at the setpoint**, add a little `kD` (a tenth of `kP` is a starting ratio) and re-check the step. Raise until the oscillation is damped; if the output line turns fuzzy, you have gone too far.
3. **If a steady offset remains**, do not reach for `kI`. Check `kS` and `kG` first. `kI` is the last resort, with `setIntegratorRange` so it cannot wind up.
4. **Use tunables.** Redeploying to change one number is the slowest possible loop; [Tunable Constants](/frc/tunable-constants-and-characterization) lets you edit gains from AdvantageScope while the mechanism runs, in simulation or on the robot.
5. **Then add the profile.** A step is the harshest test; the mechanism will actually be driven by a [motion profile](/frc/trapezoidal-motion-profiling), and the gains that survive the step will be comfortable under it.

</StepsBox>

(The step-doubling method is Team 3255's; their [write-up](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/frc-3255-supernurds-2025-build-thread/477499/116) is worth reading.) The full by-hand recipe FRC 4451 follows, per mechanism and with the reasons for each step, is the [Manual Tuning](/frc/manual-tuning-introduction) group that follows this one: one page of shared ground, then a recipe per mechanism.

### What PID cannot do

- **Hold against gravity without error.** That is feedforward `kG` ([Feedforward](/frc/feedforward)).
- **Reach a far setpoint smoothly.** A step saturates the output and PID has no plan for the journey; a [motion profile](/frc/trapezoidal-motion-profiling) supplies one, and [Why Motion Magic](/frc/why-motion-magic) shows the difference on a graph.
- **Control two coupled things at once**, such as a two-joint arm, or a swerve chassis where every module affects every other. For those, WPILib's [state-space controllers](https://docs.wpilib.org/en/stable/docs/software/advanced-controls/state-space/index.html) model the system as matrices (`ẋ = Ax + Bu`) and an **LQR** picks gains that trade error against effort. It is real and it is in the library; it is also not something FRC 4451's mechanisms have needed. PID with feedforward and a profile covers the elevator, the shooter, the hood, the intake and the drivetrain. Know that the next tool exists; do not reach for it until a mechanism proves it needs it.

<RulesBox title="Common mistakes">

- **Copying a <code>kP</code> from another robot.** Different units, different mechanism, meaningless.
- **Tuning PID before feedforward.** You will tune twice.
- **<code>kI</code> to fix sag.** That is <code>kG</code>'s job. The integral winds up during every long move and overshoots at the end.
- **A heading loop without continuous input.** The robot spins the long way round from 179° to −179°.
- **Tuning on a step and calling it done.** Check the response under the profile you will actually use.
- **Reading only the measurement.** Without the setpoint on the same axis, a graph cannot say whether the number is right.

</RulesBox>
