If a robot moves at 2 metres per second for 3 seconds, its position changes by 6 metres. Multiplying
a constant rate by elapsed time gives an accumulated change. An integral extends that idea to a rate
that varies.

Read [Functions and Rates of Change](/java/fundamentals/functions-and-rates-of-change) and
[Derivatives](/java/fundamentals/derivatives) first. The optional Java example uses
[For Loops](/java/fundamentals/for-loops). You can work through the mathematics without a robot or a
programming environment.

## Add Changes over Time

Begin with a made-up velocity trace. Positive velocity means forward along one chosen axis. Assume
each velocity is constant throughout its listed interval:

<ContentTable title="Signed Changes in Position" headers={["Time interval (s)", "Velocity (m/s)", "Duration (s)", "Position change (m)"]} rows={[
  ["0 to 1", "2", "1", "2"],
  ["1 to 2", "3", "1", "3"],
  ["2 to 3", "−1", "1", "−1"]
]} />

Multiply velocity by duration in each row, then add the changes. The **displacement**, or signed
change in position, is <Equation tex="2+3-1=4" /> metres. The **distance travelled** is
<Equation tex="2+3+1=6" /> metres, because distance counts travel in either direction positively.

On a velocity-versus-time graph, each interval forms a rectangle. Its height is velocity and its
width is duration. A rectangle below zero contributes negatively to displacement. “Area under a
curve” in this context means **signed area**, not automatically a positive geometric area.

The units also multiply: metres per second times seconds gives metres. For another signal, the
accumulated units will be that signal's units times seconds.

## Approximate a Changing Velocity

Suppose the numerical velocity model is <Equation tex="v(t)=2t" />, with time in seconds and
velocity in metres per second, from zero through three seconds. Unlike the previous table, velocity
changes within each one-second interval.

If we use the velocity at the **left endpoint**, the start of each interval, the rectangle heights
are 0, 2, and 4. Their areas add to 6 metres. Using the right endpoints gives heights 2, 4, and 6
and a total of 12 metres. These are approximations; neither assumption matches the changing line
within an interval.

<CalculusGraph kind="area" />

For this increasing velocity, the left rectangles lie below the line and the right rectangles lie
above it. The exact signed area is a triangle with base 3 seconds and height 6 metres per second.
Half the base times the height gives 9 metres.

Using six equal intervals instead of three gives a left sum of 7.5 metres and a right sum of 10.5
metres. The narrower rectangles follow the line more closely. This lower-versus-upper comparison
relies on the function increasing over the interval; it does not hold for every changing signal.

## Read the Integral Symbol

A sum of rectangle contributions is called a **Riemann sum**. For a continuous function on a finite
interval, these sums approach a definite value as the largest interval width approaches zero. That
value is the **definite integral**:

<Equation display tex="\int_a^b v(t)\,\mathrm{d}t" />

The elongated <Equation tex="\int" /> means integration. The lower and upper limits,
<Equation tex="a" /> and <Equation tex="b" />, identify the starting and ending times. The
expression <Equation tex="v(t)" /> is the function being accumulated, called the **integrand**. The
final <Equation tex="\mathrm{d}t" /> says we integrate with respect to time.

For our model, the notation and result are:

<Equation display tex="\int_0^3 2t\,\mathrm{d}t=9" />

The result is a displacement of 9 metres. An integral of velocity gives a change in position. To
obtain the final position, add the initial position: starting at 5 metres gives a final position of
14 metres in this example.

## Antiderivatives and the Fundamental Theorem

An **antiderivative** reverses a differentiation step. If the derivative of <Equation tex="F(t)" />
is <Equation tex="f(t)" />, then <Equation tex="F" /> is an antiderivative of <Equation tex="f" />.

The derivative of <Equation tex="t^2" /> is <Equation tex="2t" />. The functions
<Equation tex="t^2+5" /> and <Equation tex="t^2-8" /> have that same derivative because constants
differentiate to zero. We express this family using an **indefinite integral**:

<Equation display tex="\int 2t\,\mathrm{d}t=t^2+C" />

The constant <Equation tex="C" /> is any constant value. An initial condition can determine it. If
position is 5 metres at time zero, the corresponding numerical position model is
<Equation tex="t^2+5" />. The indefinite integral describes a family of functions; a definite
integral over fixed bounds gives a number.

The **Fundamental Theorem of Calculus** connects antiderivatives to accumulation. If
<Equation tex="f" /> is continuous from <Equation tex="a" /> through <Equation tex="b" />, and
<Equation tex="F'=f" />, then:

<Equation display tex="\int_a^b f(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=F(b)-F(a)" />

Evaluate the antiderivative at the upper bound and subtract its value at the lower bound. For the
velocity model, this gives <Equation tex="3^2-0^2=9" />, matching the triangular area. The constant
cancels, so it is not needed when evaluating the definite integral.

The other direction of the theorem says that the accumulation function
<Equation tex="A(t)=\int_a^t f(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau" /> has derivative
<Equation tex="A'(t)=f(t)" /> at interior points where the continuous-function conditions hold. The
Greek letter <Equation tex="\tau" />, “tau,” is a temporary time variable inside the integral;
<Equation tex="t" /> specifies where to stop accumulating.

This explains the connection: differentiating accumulated displacement returns velocity, while
integrating velocity recovers displacement. The starting position supplies the information a
derivative cannot preserve.

## Integrate Samples in Java

Recorded measurements often have no known formula to integrate symbolically. We can still add
approximate contributions. A left-endpoint sum uses the previous sample throughout each interval. A
**trapezoidal** estimate uses the average of the two endpoint values:

<Equation display tex="\Delta x\approx\frac{v_{\text{previous}}+v_{\text{current}}}{2}\,\Delta t" />

A trapezoid is the four-sided region under the straight line joining the two endpoint heights. The
estimate is exact for a velocity that varies linearly over the interval; it is generally approximate
for a curve.

This complete program compares both estimates for the model <Equation tex="v(t)=2t" /> over three
seconds:

```java
public class Main {
  public static void main(String[] args) {
    int intervals = 3;
    double durationSeconds = 3.0;
    double intervalSeconds = durationSeconds / intervals;
    double leftSumMeters = 0.0;
    double trapezoidSumMeters = 0.0;

    for (int i = 0; i < intervals; i++) {
      double startSeconds = i * intervalSeconds;
      double endSeconds = (i + 1) * intervalSeconds;
      double startVelocity = 2.0 * startSeconds;
      double endVelocity = 2.0 * endSeconds;

      leftSumMeters += startVelocity * intervalSeconds;
      trapezoidSumMeters += (startVelocity + endVelocity) / 2.0 * intervalSeconds;
    }

    System.out.println(leftSumMeters);
    System.out.println(trapezoidSumMeters);
  }
}
```

Select **Run**, or use [Java Setup](/java/fundamentals/setup) to run it as `Main.java`. Expected
output:

```text
6.0
9.0
```

Change `intervals` to 6. The outputs become `7.5` and `9.0`. The trapezoidal result stays exact here
because the velocity model is linear. This program assumes a positive interval count. For a real
log, use its timestamp differences and check missing or out-of-order samples. Repeatedly adding a
biased sensor reading also accumulates its bias; integration does not remove measurement errors.

## The I Term in PID

PID error is setpoint minus measurement. The integral term accumulates signed error over time and
scales it by a gain:

<Equation display tex="u_I(t)=k_I\int_0^t e(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau" />

Here time zero is the chosen start or reset of accumulation. For position error in metres, the
integral has units of metre-seconds. To produce volts, <Equation tex="k_I" />, written `kI` in code,
has units of volts per metre-second.

Suppose an illustrative controller has a constant positive error of 0.10 metres for 2 seconds. Its
accumulated error is 0.20 metre-seconds. A gain of 0.5 volts per metre-second would produce +0.10
volts of I contribution. Twice the error for half the time gives the same accumulation, which is why
I depends on both error size and duration. These values are arithmetic examples, not recommended
gains.

A sampled rectangular update has this form:

<Equation display tex="S_{\text{new}}=S_{\text{old}}+e_{\text{sample}}\Delta t" />

The stored sum <Equation tex="S" /> is the accumulated error. Multiplying by elapsed time matters:
adding error alone makes the result depend on how often the code runs. Controllers can differ in
which sample they use, how they limit accumulation, and when they reset it; follow the API's
contract.

If error becomes zero, it stops adding to this sum. It does **not** erase the stored value. A stored
integral contribution can therefore keep providing output at zero current error. Negative error
reduces a positive accumulation and can eventually make it negative. An integral is signed memory of
error, not simply a timer that resets whenever the error changes.

This memory can also cause **integral windup**. If the requested output is already beyond what the
actuator can deliver, error may persist while the integral keeps growing. The accumulated
contribution can then continue pushing after the mechanism reaches its target. WPILib provides an
integral-contribution limit and an error zone to constrain accumulation. Use those controls
deliberately; adding I is not a substitute for checking units, mechanism limits, or missing
feedforward.

## Put P, I, and D Together

An ideal continuous PID calculation with zero accumulated error at the chosen starting time is:

<Equation display tex="u(t)=k_P e(t)+k_I\int_0^t e(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau+k_D\frac{\mathrm{d}e}{\mathrm{d}t}" />

P responds to the current error. I responds to accumulated signed error. D responds to the error's
current rate of change. Each gain converts a different quantity into the same output units so the
terms can be added. A running controller uses sampled approximations and may apply additional
limits.

Continue to [PID Control](/frc/pid-control#the-three-terms) for the robot-control context, including
[Feedforward](/frc/feedforward). Understanding I and D explains what the gains act on; it does not
determine safe gains for a mechanism.

## Check Your Understanding

1. A robot moves forward at 1.5 metres per second for 2 seconds, then backward at 1 metre per second
   for 1 second. Find displacement and distance travelled.
2. Evaluate <Equation tex="\int_1^3 2t\,\mathrm{d}t" /> using an antiderivative.
3. Explain the difference between <Equation tex="\int 2t\,\mathrm{d}t" /> and
   <Equation tex="\int_0^3 2t\,\mathrm{d}t" />.
4. Error stays at −0.05 metres for 4 seconds. Find the accumulated error and its units.
5. Explain why reaching zero error does not necessarily make the I contribution zero.
