A position reading tells you where a robot is. Two readings taken at different times also tell you
how quickly its position changed. Calculus develops that connection between a quantity, its rate of
change, and its accumulated change.

This lesson starts from arithmetic. You can work through it on paper without Java or a robot.
[Calculations](/java/fundamentals/calculations) and [Math](/java/fundamentals/math) are useful
preparation for the Java examples on the next two pages.

The sequence follows selected foundations from Frank Thorne's USC
[MATH 141: Calculus I](https://thornef.github.io/math141/index.html): functions and graphs, rates
and limits, derivatives, then integrals. These are original robotics examples, not a replacement for
the full university course.

## A Function Connects an Input to an Output

A **function** assigns exactly one output to each allowed input. We can name a function
<Equation tex="f" /> and write <Equation tex="f(t)" />, read as “f of t.” The parentheses identify
the input. They do not mean that we multiply the letters together.

Consider a made-up position model:

<Equation display tex="x(t)=1+2t" />

Here, <Equation tex="t" /> is the numerical time in seconds, and <Equation tex="x(t)" /> is the
numerical position in metres along one straight axis. Positive position points forward from a chosen
starting reference. The expression <Equation tex="2t" /> means two times <Equation tex="t" />. In
Java, write multiplication explicitly as `2.0 * timeSeconds`.

To find the position at three seconds, replace every <Equation tex="t" /> with 3:

<Equation display tex="x(3)=1+2(3)=7" />

The model therefore predicts a position of 7 metres at that time. In a formula that carries units
explicitly, the starting position is 1 metre and the coefficient of time is 2 metres per second.
Multiplying that rate by a time produces metres.

The allowed inputs form the function's **domain**. For this example, suppose the model applies from
zero through three seconds. We write that interval as <Equation tex="0\le t\le3" />. The symbol
<Equation tex="\le" /> means “less than or equal to.” A formula outside its stated interval may
describe mathematics that the real mechanism cannot follow.

## Read a Table and a Graph

Evaluate the same formula at several times:

<ContentTable title="A Constant-Rate Position Model" headers={["Time (s)", "Position (m)"]} rows={[
  ["0", "1"], ["1", "3"], ["2", "5"], ["3", "7"]
]} />

A graph places time on the horizontal axis and position on the vertical axis. The ordered pair
<Equation tex="(2,5)" /> means “at two seconds, the position is five metres.” It is a time-position
pair, not an X-Y location on the field.

On paper, label those axes and their units, then plot the four rows. The points lie on a straight
line. This model predicts every intermediate time too: at 1.5 seconds, the position is 4 metres. A
sensor log only contains its recorded samples; drawing a line between them is an assumption about
what happened between readings.

## Calculate an Average Rate

Between two times, subtract the earlier position from the later position. Then divide by the elapsed
time:

<Equation display tex="\text{average velocity}=\frac{\text{later position}-\text{earlier position}}{\text{later time}-\text{earlier time}}" />

From one to three seconds in the table, the position changes from 3 metres to 7 metres:

<Equation display tex="\frac{7-3}{3-1}=2\;\mathrm{m/s}" />

The graph's **slope** is the change up the vertical axis divided by the change along the horizontal
axis. On a position-versus-time graph, slope has units of metres per second and describes velocity.
Velocity includes a sign: a negative value means motion along the negative direction of the chosen
axis. Speed is the magnitude of velocity; it does not include that direction sign.

The Greek letter <Equation tex="\Delta" />, pronounced “delta,” is shorthand for a change.
Subscripts distinguish the earlier reading, numbered 1, from the later reading, numbered 2:

<Equation display tex="\Delta t=t_2-t_1,\qquad\Delta x=x(t_2)-x(t_1)" />

<Equation display tex="\text{average velocity}=\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}" />

Keep the subtraction order the same in the numerator and denominator. The two timestamps need to
differ; dividing by zero does not define a rate.

## A Changing Rate

Now use a different numerical model, <Equation tex="x(t)=t^2" />, again with time in seconds and
position in metres. The exponent 2 means to multiply the input by itself. The implied coefficient is
1 metre per second squared. This is an invented model for a short interval, not a measured robot
trajectory.

<ContentTable title="Position for a Changing Rate" headers={["Time (s)", "Position (m)", "Average velocity since the previous row (m/s)"]} rows={[
  ["0", "0", "No previous reading"], ["1", "1", "1"], ["2", "4", "3"], ["3", "9", "5"]
]} />

The intervals all last one second, but the changes in position grow. Connecting all four points with
one straight line would miss that changing rate.

Across the entire three seconds, the average velocity is 3 metres per second. That average does not
tell us the velocity at every instant. In particular, it does not establish that the robot was
moving at 3 metres per second when the clock read two seconds.

## Smaller Intervals and Limits

To study the rate near two seconds, keep the first time at 2 and move the second time closer to it.
For the quadratic model, an interval from 2 to 3 seconds gives 5 metres per second. From 2 to 2.5
seconds it gives 4.5 metres per second. From 2 to 2.25 seconds it gives 4.25 metres per second.

Those rates approach 4 as the interval gets smaller. A **limit** describes a value approached as an
input approaches a specified value. The next lesson uses a limit of average rates to define the rate
at an instant, called a **derivative**. We do not obtain it by substituting a zero time interval
into a division.

Measured data adds a complication: shorter intervals also make small position errors more noticeable
in the calculated rate. A mathematical limit and a calculation from noisy samples are related ideas,
but they are different operations.

## Check Your Understanding

1. Evaluate <Equation tex="f(t)=4+3t" /> at <Equation tex="t=2" />. Explain the role of the
   parentheses and the multiplication.
2. A position changes from 5 metres to 2 metres over 1.5 seconds. Find the average velocity and
   explain its sign.
3. For <Equation tex="x(t)=t^2" />, calculate the average rate from 1 to 2 seconds, then from 1 to
   1.5 seconds. Explain why the answers differ.
4. Explain why two readings with the same timestamp cannot provide a rate by division.
