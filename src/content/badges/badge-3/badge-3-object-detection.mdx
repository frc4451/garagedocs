The student builds a camera input path for one stationary game piece and uses observations to choose
an acquisition pose. Start from the vision package in the student's `Badge3Starter` fork and from
the student's Badge 2 pose and AprilTag work. Observation IO and geometry can proceed alongside
mechanism refinement; Step 12 requires this step and the Step 9 subsystem states together.

Read these first:

- [Pose Geometry](/frc/pose-geometry)
- [From Camera to Field](/frc/from-camera-to-field)
- [PhotonVision](/frc/photonvision)
- [Vision Subsystem](/frc/vision-subsystem)
- [Vision Simulation](/frc/vision-simulation)

## Build the IO Boundary

Start from Cobra's `ObjectDetectionIO`, `ObjectDetectionIOPhoton`, and `ObjectDetectionIOPhotonSim`
in the fork. `ObjectDetectionIOPhoton` already reads `getAllUnreadResults()` and stamps each
observation with its result timestamp, and `FuelTracker` forgets observations older than a tunable
tracking memory. The simulated implementation extends the real implementation and supplies a
`PhotonCameraSim`, following the same pattern used for AprilTags. Both modes consume PhotonVision
results through the real parsing path. REPLAY reads recorded inputs without constructing cameras or
field physics.

The fork's `ObjectDetectionVision` picks its IO inside its own constructor and updates the shared
`VisionConstants.objectDetectionSim` from `BobotState.getGlobalPose()`, the estimated pose. Move
both choices out: choose the object-detection IO and the SIM camera world in `RobotContainer`, as
Badge 2 did for AprilTags in [Architecture](/badges/badge-2-photonvision#architecture), and pass
both to `ObjectDetectionVision`.

Badge 2's [simulated camera world](/badges/badge-2-photonvision#simulation) was rendered from the
estimated pose, which is why it could not show vision correcting a wrong estimate. Until Step 11
adds a true pose, the camera world keeps rendering from the estimated pose; record that in the log.
Passing the pose as a supplier lets Step 11 swap the source without changing
`ObjectDetectionVision`. Before Step 11, write down what the object-detection camera could reveal if
it rendered the field from the true pose instead.

Extend the input record so it contains connection state, observation timestamps, target yaw and
pitch, available class/confidence information, and rejection reasons. The fork's
`ObjectDetectionIOInputs` holds connection state, an object count, projected poses and target areas,
and none of the rest. Find what PhotonLib reports for class and confidence when the pipeline has no
detection model, and log that value unchanged rather than an invented label.

Follow [Process Unread Results Once](/frc/vision-subsystem#process-unread-results-once). An empty
result means no target in that frame; no new frame means the age of the previous observation
continues increasing. Clear or expire targets deliberately rather than treating an old result as
current.

Plan an entry point that a test can call without a camera: for example, a method that
takes a list of `PhotonPipelineResult` packets and fills the inputs, so recorded packets can drive
it directly. [A Pure Logic Test](/frc/unit-tests-in-simulation#a-pure-logic-test) shows the test
shape.

## Turn a Measurement into a Location

Cobra's `ObjectDetectionIOPhoton` estimates distance from the target's pitch with
`PhotonUtils.calculateDistanceToTargetMeters`, divides by the cosine of yaw, and places the result
from the current robot pose. Replace that with a ray intersection.

A camera bearing (the target's yaw and pitch) identifies a ray, not a complete three-dimensional
position. For the stationary floor-piece exercise, a known target-center height provides the
missing constraint. FuelSim's fuel radius is 0.075 m (about 3 in), so a grounded fuel's center sits
that high; the plane at that height is the **fuel-center plane**. Transform the full camera ray into
the field with the capture-time planar robot pose and the configured camera translation and
rotation, then intersect it with the fuel-center plane. Record the assumptions that make the
estimate possible.

Use `FieldToRobot`, `RobotToCamera`, and `CameraToPiece` names when drawing the transforms, as in
[From Camera to Field](/frc/from-camera-to-field). The projection uses the robot pose at the
observation timestamp;
[Prepare for Moving Observations](/badges/badge-3-object-detection#prepare-for-moving-observations)
covers where that pose comes from on a moving
robot.

List the geometric cases where an intersection is meaningless and give each a logged reason. Include
values that are not finite numbers, camera and plane heights that make an intersection impossible,
rays that never reach the plane in front of the camera, and points the field cannot contain. Start
with a provisional range limit, log it, and replace it with one the
[Physical Camera Check](/badges/badge-3-object-detection#physical-camera-check) supports. Read
[Reject Measurements for Stated Reasons](/frc/vision-subsystem#reject-measurements-for-stated-reasons)
for the AprilTag version of this rule. A mounting transform that looks right but is wrong still
produces plausible wrong positions; known-location measurements are needed to detect it.

Check the geometry with a known target. Place one simulated target straight ahead of the camera,
then move it to one side. Predict the sign of yaw before running it, verify the library's yaw
convention, and compare the estimated field position with the configured simulation position. Log
the estimated piece position and the configured target position separately. The selection logic
sees only the estimate.

Before trusting that comparison, find the height of the fork's simulated fuel.
`Robot.simulationInit` adds ten placeholder `VisionTargetSim` fuel targets to
`VisionConstants.objectDetectionSim`, each a `TargetModel` cuboid whose pose has `z = 0`. Read the
`TargetModel` cuboid constructor to find where the target's center sits relative to that pose.
Predict the range error the intersection makes when it assumes the fuel-center plane, run the check,
and compare. Until the fuel-world adapter from
[Model the Camera in Simulation](/badges/badge-3-object-detection#model-the-camera-in-simulation)
replaces these placeholders, move them so their centers sit at the fuel radius.

## Log the Projection

Log each frame's timestamp, sequence number, reason and capture-time robot pose, and each
observation's raw bearings, class/confidence, frame index and reason. Log the camera transform,
target height and range limit the projection used. Keep three frame cases distinct: no new frame, a
received image with no targets, and a frame without capture-pose history. The last is a rejection,
not a target at a placeholder zero pose. Test the parser with recorded packets and check that the
raw inputs round-trip through the log.

Normal REPLAY uses saved projected inputs, as in
[Check the Replay Boundary](/frc/vision-subsystem#check-the-replay-boundary). To investigate a
projection change, recompute from the saved raw bearings, frame-indexed capture pose, camera
transform, height, and range in a separate comparison. Older logs without these fields
cannot support that raw-bearing analysis.

## Prepare for Moving Observations

The stationary physical camera check establishes geometry without demonstrating motion compensation.
Before Step 12, the student documents how each observation is matched to a robot pose during the
simulated approach. Use pose history at capture time when available, or stop and obtain a fresh
stationary observation before calculating a field target. State the permitted observation age and
motion conditions; reject frames outside them. A current-pose calculation on a moving robot requires
a quantified latency-error bound and a justified acceptance limit.

The history already exists. `Drive`'s `globalPoseEstimator` is a `SwerveDrivePoseEstimator`, and
WPILib's `PoseEstimator.sampleAt` returns the estimated pose at a past timestamp from that
estimator's buffer. Read [Fused Pose Estimation](/frc/fused-pose-estimation) for how the estimator
is advanced and corrected, then add a `Drive` method that samples it for the camera IO. Choose a
maximum sample age, justify it from the camera latency and the robot's speed during the approach,
and decide what happens to a frame captured before a pose reset.

Test a delayed frame during simulated motion and show the compensation or rejection decision in the
log. This delayed-frame test belongs to the observation evidence in B3-10.2. The target remains a
stationary piece; the robot may move.

## Select an Acquisition Pose

Track the single stationary piece with an expiry time. Select a valid target using a documented
rule. Calculate the acquisition pose: the robot pose that puts the intake at the piece, including
the robot-to-intake offset and the desired heading. Driving the robot center onto the piece is not
equivalent to aligning the intake.

Find which face of the robot the intake is on. `VisionConstants` mounts the intake camera,
`DangerCam`, with a yaw of 180 degrees, and `FuelClusterVARC` adds 180 degrees to its rotation
target. Explain what each of those means, then state the robot heading that points the intake at a
piece and check it against a drawing of the robot on the field.

Test no target, stale target, out-of-bounds position, near-horizontal ray, and a deliberately wrong
mounting transform. Save both accepted and rejected observations. The mentor chooses a different
camera transform for an independent prediction and correction.

## Physical Camera Check

The provided coprocessor and color camera already run PhotonVision with a working pipeline.
Installation and model preparation are supplied setup. The student owns the robot-side IO,
transform configuration, validity rules, tracking and selection.

Before the check, record the coprocessor, camera and measured mounting transform on the
[Step 13 setup sheet](/badges/badge-3-hardware#complete-the-setup-sheet). If the robot is enabled
during the check, follow [First Enable](/badges/badge-3-hardware#first-enable) first.

Use the provided Orange Pi 5 or Rubik Pi 3 and camera with the mentor present. Measure the mounting
transform as in [Measure robotToCamera](/frc/pose-estimation-calibration#5-measure-robottocamera)
and place the stationary target at several known locations. Compare observations and calculated
positions with tape measurements. Record lighting, pipeline, target type, distances, timestamp
behavior and limitations. Replace the provisional range limit with one these distances support.
This demonstration does not require autonomous motion on hardware.

## Model the Camera in Simulation

After Step 6's fuel world exists, build the adapter that drives the camera world's targets from the
fuel world, replacing the placeholder targets. This adapter and its capture-time scenes are B3-10.2
evidence. Start with grounded fuel only, because the projection assumes a grounded fuel center.
Leaving airborne fuel out is a simulation simplification; the robot code does not classify fuel by
height.

A captured image must keep its original scene and timestamp while it waits for delivery, so motion
or collection after capture cannot rewrite an older image. Read `VisionSystemSim.update` in
PhotonLib v2026.1.1 (in Sources). Find which robot pose and which target list it uses when it
renders a delayed frame. Then decide what the student's camera world must change so that a piece
collected after capture still appears in the older image and nowhere later. Step 12 adds collection
and the removal of collected targets; this step makes the capture-time scene ready for it.

Test moving fuel and robot poses through PhotonCamera, the ordinary object-detection IO, and the
normal Drive estimator history. Approximate spatial tracks can merge nearby fuel, so the track count
is not a proven fuel count.

## Heading Assistance Checkpoint

After single-piece tracking, start from Cobra's driver controller (port 0) A-button binding, which
is separate from the operator controller's A-button feed binding in Steps 1 and 9. It runs
`DriveCommands.joystickDriveAtAngle` toward `BobotState.getFuelClusterVARC().getRotationTarget()`
while the driver keeps translation control. This checkpoint requires heading assistance; driving
automatically to a cluster is outside the required work.

Read [VARC](/frc/varc) for how that target is updated, then answer three questions from the code:

- What is the rotation target when no cluster is observed?
- What happens when A and the right bumper are both held?
- `FuelClusterVARC` sorts clusters largest first. What does that rule do with two clusters of equal
  size?

Keep driver translation active while the heading turns toward the selected cluster, document the
selection rule and its tie-break, and demonstrate cluster selection, the fallback when no cluster is
observed, and the stop when A is released. Test the A-button binding for heading assistance, driver
translation, and target loss. Save the heading-assist log with the B3-10.2 evidence, together with a
short comparison of the student's selection rule against Cobra's largest-cluster rule on the same
scene. As an option, repeat the heading-assist demonstration with moving fuel.
