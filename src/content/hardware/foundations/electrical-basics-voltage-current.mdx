### Start with three quantities

Every powered device has an input-voltage requirement and a current demand. Use these three quantities to describe the electrical load:

- **Voltage**, measured in volts (`V`), is the electrical potential difference between two points. A device must receive a voltage it is designed to accept.
- **Current**, measured in amperes (`A`), is the flow of electric charge through a working circuit. A device draws current according to its design and operating state.
- **Power**, measured in watts (`W`), is the rate at which the device uses electrical energy.

For a direct-current load:

```text
power = voltage x current
P = V x I
```

A `5 V` device drawing `3 A` uses about `15 W`. A `12 V` device drawing `2 A` uses about `24 W`. Startup, motor load, USB devices, and processing load can make current change while the robot runs.

### Match voltage first

The FRC battery is called a **12 V battery**, but its terminal voltage is not fixed at exactly 12 V. It changes with charge, load, wiring resistance, and battery condition. A direct battery branch therefore does not provide regulated 12 V.

A **regulated output** holds a target voltage across an allowed range of input voltage and load. Some regulators only step voltage down. Others can step it up and down. Read the exact regulator documentation before choosing one.

| Label on the load | Supply to use | Example from this curriculum |
|---|---|---|
| `5 V` input | A regulated 5 V output with enough current capacity | Orange Pi 5 and the team's installed ZebraSwitch assembly |
| `12 V` input designed for regulated 12 V | A regulated 12 V output with enough current capacity | Use only when the product documentation requires regulated 12 V |
| `12 V` or battery input designed for an FRC robot | The protected robot-battery path allowed by the product documentation and current rules | roboRIO, power distribution, and supported robot hardware |

The words **12 V** alone are not enough to select a connection. Check whether the product accepts raw robot-battery voltage or requires regulated 12 V.

### Current is demand, capacity, and protection

A supply's current rating states how much current it can provide within its limits. It does not force that full current through a healthy load.

For example, a regulated `5 V, 5 A` supply can normally power a `5 V` device that draws `2 A`. The voltage matches, and the supply has enough capacity. A `5 V, 1 A` supply is undersized for that load and may drop voltage, overheat, shut down, or reset the device.

Current capacity does not replace circuit protection. The breaker or fuse, wire size, connectors, regulator, and load must form one safe circuit that follows the current FRC rules. A breaker protects the branch wiring from excessive current. It does not convert voltage or guarantee that an electronic device cannot be damaged.

### Loads add together

Robot branch circuits are generally connected in parallel. Each branch receives its supply voltage, while the currents add at the source.

```text
5 V camera load:       1.0 A
5 V coprocessor load:  3.0 A
                       -----
minimum combined load: 4.0 A, before startup margin and regulator limits
```

Do not add voltage ratings for parallel loads. Add their expected currents, account for peak demand, and check how much current the regulator can deliver at the robot's input voltage and expected temperature.

### Recognize common failures

| Problem | Likely result |
|---|---|
| Supply voltage is too high | Immediate or delayed device damage |
| Supply voltage is too low | Resets, failure to boot, or unstable communication |
| Supply current capacity is too low | Voltage drop, regulator shutdown, heat, or repeated brownouts |
| Polarity is reversed | Device or regulator damage unless the product protects against it |
| Loose or undersized connection | Voltage drop, heat, intermittent resets, or arcing |
| Unprotected conductor shorts | Current can rise until wiring or another component fails |

When a coprocessor works on the bench but resets during acceleration, measure its input voltage during the event. The drivetrain's current can lower battery voltage, and resistance in the power path can cause an additional voltage drop.

### Use a meter without creating a short

Use a multimeter in **DC voltage mode** across the positive and negative points you want to compare. Confirm the meter leads are in the voltage and common jacks before touching the circuit.

Never place a meter configured for current measurement directly across a battery, regulator output, or other voltage source. That connection creates a short circuit. A student should measure current only with the approved team procedure and mentor supervision.

Before attaching a new electronic load:

1. Read the device input-voltage range and peak-current requirement.
2. Read the regulator input range, output voltage, and current-versus-temperature limits.
3. Select legal branch protection, conductors, and connectors.
4. Check polarity and continuity with robot power off.
5. Power the circuit without the load and measure its output voltage.
6. Turn power off, attach the load, and inspect the connection again.
7. Power the load and watch voltage, temperature, and behavior under realistic robot load.
